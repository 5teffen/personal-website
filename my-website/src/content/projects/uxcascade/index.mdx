## The question

AI tools now let designers produce many versions of an interface in minutes, but testing each version with real people still takes days. Language model agents can stand in for users, browse a site with a goal and a persona, and explain what they are doing at every step. The problem is what comes out. A few dozen agent runs produce long action logs and reasoning traces that are tedious to read and hard to act on.

We asked how to turn that output into findings a UX practitioner can use.

## What we built

UXCascade structures agent output into a five-stage workflow<Sidenote>The stages come from a formative study with UX practitioners, who asked for fast iteration, feedback from specific personas, issues tied to user goals, quick reruns and help prioritizing.</Sidenote>.

1. **Explore** the goals agents pursued and which of them failed.
2. **Observe** how failures spread across persona traits such as age or new versus returning visitors.
3. **Isolate** concrete issues, each linked to the agent's reasoning, the page element and the step in its journey.
4. **Propose** a fix by editing the page directly.
5. **Evaluate** the fix by replaying the agents on the edited page.

## How well it works

Eight UX professionals, with 6.5 years of experience on average, looked for problems in a mock clothing shop seeded with 17 usability issues. Each used UXCascade and, in counterbalanced order, a usability report compiled from ten lay testers.

Participants found slightly more seeded issues with UXCascade than with the report, 2.9 against 2.6 on average, though the difference was not significant with eight people. The agents on their own surfaced 9 of the 17 issues and the human report 11. They found different ones. The report was stronger on interaction problems, and the agents matched or beat it on communication and visual consistency, so simulation works best alongside human feedback rather than instead of it.
